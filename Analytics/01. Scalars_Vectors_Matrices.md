## Key Concepts

### 1. Scalars

A **scalar** is a single numerical value. It's a quantity that only has magnitude. In the context of data, a scalar could be a single data point, a specific measurement, or a constant value.

* **Examples:**
    * The temperature: $25^\circ C$
    * Your age: $30$
    * The price of an item: $9.99$
    * A model's learning rate: $0.01$

* **Notation:** Scalars are typically represented by lowercase italic letters, like $a$, $b$, $\alpha$, or $\lambda$.

---

### 2. Vectors

A **vector** is an ordered list of numbers. These numbers can represent various things, such as:

* A point in space.
* A direction and magnitude.
* A list of features for a single data point (e.g., in a dataset, each row might be a vector representing an observation).

Vectors have both **magnitude** (length) and **direction**.

* **Representation:** Vectors are commonly written as **column vectors** or **row vectors**.
    * **Column Vector:**
        $$
        \mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ v_3 \end{pmatrix}
        $$
    * **Row Vector:**
        $$
        \mathbf{v}^T = \begin{pmatrix} v_1 & v_2 & v_3 \end{pmatrix}
        $$
    (Note: The $^T$ denotes the **transpose**, turning a column vector into a row vector and vice versa.)

* **Dimension:** The number of elements in a vector is its dimension. A vector with $n$ elements is an $n$-dimensional vector. For example, $\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$ is a 2-dimensional vector.

* **Geometric Interpretation:** In a 2D or 3D coordinate system, a vector can be visualized as an arrow starting from the origin $(0,0)$ and ending at the point defined by its components. The length of the arrow is its magnitude, and its orientation is its direction.

* **Examples:**
    * A point in 2D space: $\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$
    * Features of a person: $\begin{pmatrix} \text{height} \\ \text{weight} \\ \text{age} \end{pmatrix}$

* **Notation:** Vectors are usually denoted by lowercase bold letters, like $\mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{x}$, or $\mathbf{y}$. Individual elements are denoted with subscripts, e.g., $v_1, v_2$.

---

### 3. Matrices

A **matrix** is a rectangular array of numbers, organized into rows and columns. Matrices are fundamental for representing collections of data, transformations, and relationships between different entities.

* **Representation:** A matrix is typically enclosed in large parentheses or brackets.
    $$
    \mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix}
    a_{11} & a_{12} & \dots & a_{1n} \\
    a_{21} & a_{22} & \dots & a_{2n} \\
    \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    a_{m1} & a_{m2} & \dots & a_{mn}
    \end{pmatrix}
    $$
* **Dimensions:** A matrix with $m$ rows and $n$ columns is called an $m \times n$ matrix. The element in the $i$-th row and $j$-th column is denoted by $a_{ij}$.

* **Examples:**
    * A dataset where rows are observations and columns are features:
        $$
        \begin{pmatrix}
        1.75 & 70 & 25 \\
        1.60 & 55 & 32 \\
        1.85 & 80 & 28
        \end{pmatrix}
        $$
        (Here, height, weight, age for 3 different people).
    * An image can be represented as a matrix of pixel intensity values.

* **Notation:** Matrices are generally represented by uppercase bold letters, like $\mathbf{A}$, $\mathbf{B}$, or $\mathbf{M}$.

temp_celsius = 28.5
print(f"Scalar: {temp_celsius}, Type: {type(temp_celsius)}")

# Column vector (NumPy convention: 2D array with 1 column)
