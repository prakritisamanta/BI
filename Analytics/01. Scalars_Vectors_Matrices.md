## Key Concepts

### 1. Scalars

A **scalar** is simply a single numerical value. Think of it as a quantity that has only **magnitude** (size or amount) but no direction. In the world of data and AI, a scalar might represent a single measurement, a count, a temperature reading, or a specific coefficient in a mathematical equation.

* **Examples:**
    * The speed of a car: $60 \text{ mph}$
    * The number of items in a shopping cart: $5$
    * A single weight in a neural network: $0.72$
    * The temperature: $28.5^\circ C$

* **Notation:** Scalars are commonly denoted by lowercase italic letters, like $a$, $b$, $x$, or Greek letters such as $\alpha$ (alpha) or $\lambda$ (lambda).

---

### 2. Vectors

A **vector** is an ordered list of numbers. Unlike a scalar, a vector possesses both **magnitude** (length) and **direction**. Vectors are incredibly versatile and can represent:

* **Points in space:** For example, a location $(x, y, z)$ in 3D.
* **Directions and movements:** The displacement from one point to another.
* **Features of a data point:** In a dataset, each row often represents an observation, and the values in that row form a vector of features (e.g., a person's height, weight, and age).

* **Representation:** Vectors are typically written as a list of numbers, either vertically as a **column vector** or horizontally as a **row vector**.

    * **Column Vector:** This is the most common default representation in many linear algebra contexts.
        $$
        \mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ v_3 \end{pmatrix}
        $$

    * **Row Vector:** A column vector can be transformed into a row vector (and vice-versa) by taking its **transpose**, denoted by a superscript $^T$.
        $$
        \mathbf{v}^T = \begin{pmatrix} v_1 & v_2 & v_3 \end{pmatrix}
        $$

* **Dimension:** The **dimension** of a vector is simply the number of elements it contains. A vector with $n$ elements is an $n$-dimensional vector. For instance, $\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}$ is a 2-dimensional vector.

* **Geometric Interpretation:**
    * In a 2D coordinate system, a vector like $\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$ can be visualized as an arrow starting from the origin $(0,0)$ and ending at the point $(3,4)$. The length of this arrow is its magnitude, and its orientation indicates its direction.
    * This geometric intuition extends to 3D and even higher dimensions (though hard to visualize!), where vectors still represent points or directions in that space.
    

* **Examples:**
    * A point in 2D space: $\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}$
    * A vector representing a customer's purchasing habits (e.g., spending on groceries, electronics, clothing): $\begin{pmatrix} \$250 \\ \$75 \\ \$120 \end{pmatrix}$

* **Notation:** Vectors are generally denoted by lowercase bold letters, like $\mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{x}$, or $\mathbf{y}$. Individual elements within a vector are identified using subscripts, such as $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$.

---

### 3. Matrices

A **matrix** is a rectangular array of numbers, organized into rows and columns. Matrices are fundamental for structuring and manipulating larger collections of data, representing transformations, and encoding complex relationships.

* **Representation:** A matrix is enclosed in large parentheses or brackets. Each number inside is called an element.

    $$
    \mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix}
    a_{11} & a_{12} & \dots & a_{1n} \\
    a_{21} & a_{22} & \dots & a_{2n} \\
    \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    a_{m1} & a_{m2} & \dots & a_{mn}
    \end{pmatrix}
    $$

* **Dimensions:** The **dimensions** of a matrix are given by its number of rows ($m$) and its number of columns ($n$). We refer to it as an $m \times n$ matrix. The element located in the $i$-th row and $j$-th column is denoted by $a_{ij}$.

* **Examples:**
    * A dataset: Imagine a table where each row is an individual and each column is a feature (like height, weight, age). This entire table can be seen as a matrix.
        $$
        \begin{pmatrix}
        1.75 & 70 & 25 \\
        1.60 & 55 & 32 \\
        1.85 & 80 & 28
        \end{pmatrix}
        $$
        This is a $3 \times 3$ matrix representing height, weight, and age for 3 different people.
    * A grayscale image can be represented as a matrix where each element $a_{ij}$ corresponds to the pixel intensity at row $i$ and column $j$.
    * Color images are often represented as 3 matrices (one for Red, one for Green, one for Blue) stacked together.

* **Notation:** Matrices are generally represented by uppercase bold letters, like $\mathbf{A}$, $\mathbf{B}$, or $\mathbf{M}$.
